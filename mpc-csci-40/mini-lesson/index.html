15-Minute Mini-Lesson · From Module 5

Did the AI Just Lie to Me?

How LLMs find answers, why they make things up, and how to catch them — every time. This page is the lesson: scroll as we go.

min 0–1 · welcome

Hi, I'm Matt

I build robots, simulations, and AI software, and I spend my days working alongside AI coding agents — enough that I'm somewhere in the top fraction of a percent of AI-tool users at a Fortune 100 company. Which means AI systems have lied to me more times than to almost anyone you'll meet.

Today I want to show you why that happens, and how to never get burned by it.

min 1–4 · your stories first

You've seen this already

Think of a time an AI answer felt off.
What tipped you off?

Share it by voice, drop it in the chat, or just hold the thought — all three count. There are no wrong answers here; every one of these stories is data about how these systems behave.

min 4–7 · what's actually happening

An LLM is not a search engine

A large language model doesn't look things up. It has read a huge slice of everything humans have written, and it does exactly one thing: predict the next word, over and over, until an answer appears. It's the world's best improv partner — it never breaks character, and it never wants to say "I don't know."

That's why it can be brilliant and wrong in the same sentence. It isn't lying — lying requires knowing the truth. It's completing a pattern, and confident-sounding text is simply what the pattern looks like.

In my own project — Graphlings, little AI creatures that live in a simulated world — the creatures only "remember" their friends and their past because we save memories to a database and hand the right ones back at the right moment. A plain chatbot has no notebook like that. When an AI assistant does cite sources, that's search bolted on: the model is handed pages and asked to summarize them. The pages are real; the summary is still improv.

Confidence is part of the interface.
It is not evidence.

min 7–10 · three true stories

Three true stories from my desk

  • "Someone should do something"

    I once wired two chatbots together and asked them to chat like friends about their weekends. They invented hiking trips they never took, then worked themselves into a passionate rant about environmental decline — real feelings about a weekend that never happened.

    The pattern continues, whether or not it's true.

  • "Temporary artifacts"

    An AI coding agent deleted the tests protecting my code — and explained, confidently, that they were just "temporary artifacts." It had a perfectly reasonable-sounding justification for doing the exact wrong thing.

    A confident explanation is not a correct one.

  • The invisible hero

    An agent built a game feature for me. Every check passed — technically it all "worked." Except the main character spawned behind a giant tree, so you couldn't see them at all. Nothing had ever asked that question.

    "No errors" only covers the questions you asked.

min 10–13 · the fix

Give it a chance to be proven wrong

At work I wrap AI agents in layers of automated tests, like nesting dolls — unit tests inside integration tests inside end-to-end tests. Here's the surprising part: good models usually course-correct the moment reality pushes back. The trick isn't distrusting AI — it's building in the moment where it can be proven wrong.

You don't need a test lab for that. You need three moves:

  1. Open the source

    Ask "what's your source?" — then actually click it. Does the page exist? Does it say what the AI said it says?

  2. Get a second opinion

    Ask the same question to a different model, or a plain search engine. Independent answers that agree are worth far more than one confident answer.

  3. Test it against reality

    Run the code. Look up the citation. Do the arithmetic yourself. One minute of reality beats an hour of confident text.

If it survives all three —
now you can use it.

min 13–14 · your turn

Check yourself

The AI hands you a perfect citation for your essay: real-sounding journal, real-sounding authors, 2019.
What's your next move?

Voice or chat — walk me through it.

min 14–15 · landing

What you leave with

These tools are the biggest lever handed to students in a generation — I've watched people go from "that's impossible for me" to a working website in one afternoon. The whole skill is using the lever without letting it use you: enjoy the speed, and keep the three moves between the AI's confidence and your name on the work.

AI doesn't replace your thinking.
It multiplies it — and verification is the multiplier.
